# Your First Melody on the Keyboard: "Mary Had a Little Lamb", Right Hand, Steady

<!-- la:orientation -->
In this lesson you will go from never having played to playing a complete short melody, "Mary Had a Little Lamb", with your right hand. You will learn just enough to do that well: where the notes are, which finger plays each one, and how to keep a steady beat.

Why this tune? It is short (eight small sections, about half a minute long), it uses only four different keys, and every one of them sits under a finger without moving your hand. That leaves your attention free for the real goal: playing it **steadily**, so it sounds like music rather than a series of separate key presses. "Steadily" here means something specific that you will be able to check yourself: with a metronome (a device that clicks a steady beat) set to 60 clicks per minute, you play the whole tune **three times in a row without stopping**, every note starting on its click.

**Who this is for:** you have never played piano or keyboard, you have a 61-key keyboard at home, and you can count to four out loud. You do not need to read music; every note is written out as a letter name with a length in beats. Hands together, the left hand and reading sheet music are left for later lessons.

<!-- la:outcomes -->
By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Find any C, D, E, F, G, A or B on your keyboard using the groups of black keys, including middle C, and place your right hand in C position (thumb on middle C).
2. Sit at a comfortable height with a relaxed, curved hand and a level wrist, and notice and release tension (or stop if something hurts).
3. Count a steady beat aloud and give quarter, half and whole notes their full 1, 2 and 4 beats.
4. Play "Mary Had a Little Lamb" with your right hand, with the right notes and fingers, with a metronome at 60 beats per minute, three times in a row without stopping.
5. Fix a trouble spot with a short-loop routine and judge your own playing against clear self-checks.

Do not worry if some words in that list (middle C, C position, quarter note) are new: each one is explained when you first need it.

**The route:** first you set up your seat, your keyboard and your hand. Then you learn the keyboard's map so you can find the notes, and give your fingers numbers so you know which one plays each note. Next you learn the beat, which tells you *when* each note happens. With where, which finger and when in place, you learn the tune in short loops, join it together, switch on the metronome, and finally test yourself.

**How to use this lesson.** Do not try to do it in one sitting. A sensible plan is four or more sessions of 10–20 minutes, each with a short break in the middle:

- Session 1: setting up, the keyboard map, finger numbers and the warm-up.
- Session 2: the beat, then the first half of the tune.
- Session 3: the second half, then joining the halves.
- Session 4 and after: the metronome and the final test.

If a session goes badly, repeat it. That is normal, not failure.

## Before you press a key: your seat, your keyboard, your hand

Everything later in this lesson is done with your body, and the habits you form in the first few minutes tend to stick. So we start here, before a single note.

**Your seat and the keyboard's height.**

1. Put the keyboard on a stand or a steady table, not on your lap or a bed.
2. Sit on the front half of a chair or stool without armrests, feet flat on the floor, back upright but not stiff.
3. Sit facing the middle of the keyboard. You will find the exact spot in the next section; for now, the middle is fine.
4. Let your arms hang, then bring your right hand up onto the white keys. Your forearm should be roughly level with the keys: not sloping steeply up to reach them, not sloping steeply down. Adjust the chair height (a firm cushion works) or the stand until this is true.
5. Sit far enough back that your elbow is a little in front of your body and you can reach the keys without leaning forward.

**Your keyboard's settings.** Most home keyboards can make many sounds and play backing rhythms. For this lesson:

- Choose a piano sound (often called "Grand Piano" or "Piano 1"; many keyboards have a button that resets to it).
- Make sure any automatic rhythm, style or accompaniment is switched off, so that a key makes one plain note.
- If your keyboard has a **transpose** or **octave** button, make sure it is at its normal setting (usually shown as 0). These buttons change the sound a key makes, so with them switched on the tune would come out at the wrong pitch (higher or lower than normal).
- Set the volume to a comfortable level.
- If a pedal is plugged into the keyboard, keep your feet off it for this lesson; it makes notes keep sounding after you let go.

**Your hand.** Let your right arm hang loosely by your side for a few seconds, then look at your fingers: they curve gently by themselves. That natural curve is your starting shape. Now bring the hand onto the white keys, keeping the curve, so that:

- Each finger touches the key with its **fingertip pad**: the fleshy underside of the last joint, near the tip — not the flat underside of the whole finger.
- The **thumb** rests on the side of its tip, lightly; it does not stick out stiffly.
- The **little finger** stands on its tip too, rather than lying flat on its side.
- The **wrist** is level with the back of your hand: not sagging below the keys, not arched up high.

The hanging-arm picture gets the *relaxed* part right: no finger is pulled straight or curled tight. Where it stops working is that a hanging hand has nothing under it. At the keys, your fingers need a little more curve than when hanging, so that the fingertip pads (not the flat undersides of the fingers) land on the keys, and the hand rests lightly on the keys rather than dangling. Use it as a starting point, not a pose to freeze in.

**How to press a key.** Rest a fingertip on a white key. Let the finger move down from the big knuckle where it joins your hand, so the key goes all the way down; you will feel it stop at the bottom. Keep just enough weight on it to hold it down, then let it rise by relaxing the finger. The sound stops when the key comes up.

<!-- la:misconception -->
> **A tempting mistake: "Pressing harder makes a better or longer sound, so I should keep pushing."** It is natural to think effort equals sound. Try this: press a key, and once it has reached the bottom, push harder into it. With a piano sound, on almost every home keyboard, nothing changes: the sound was already decided by *how fast the key went down*, and once a key is at the bottom it cannot go further. (If your keyboard is "touch sensitive", a quicker press gives a louder note; if it is not, every press sounds the same.) Pushing at the bottom only tightens your hand and arm. The correct model: the key goes down, then you hold it with the least effort that keeps it down.

**Safety, which applies to the whole lesson:** stop if anything hurts — fingers, wrist, forearm, shoulder or neck. Tension is a fault to fix, not effort to push through. Every few minutes, let your hands drop into your lap, shake them out gently and let your shoulders drop. If pain comes back each time you play, stop practising and get advice from a teacher or a health professional.

## The keyboard map: black keys tell you where the letters are

You now know how to sit and how to press a key. The next problem is *which* key. At first a keyboard looks like a long row of identical white keys, but the black keys form a pattern that gives every white key an address. You need this before anything else, because the tune's notes are named by letters, and the letters are found from the black keys.

**The pattern.** Look along your keyboard. The black keys come in **groups of two** and **groups of three**, alternating all the way along: two, three, two, three, and so on.

**The letters.** Musicians name the white keys with the first seven letters of the alphabet, A B C D E F G. Going to the right, the letters run C, D, E, F, G, A, B, and then start again at C. The black-key groups tell you where each letter is:

| White key | Where it is |
|---|---|
| C | just to the left of a group of **two** black keys |
| D | in the middle of the group of two (between its two black keys) |
| E | just to the right of the group of two |
| F | just to the left of a group of **three** black keys |
| G | between the first and second black keys of the group of three |
| A | between the second and third black keys of the group of three |
| B | just to the right of the group of three |

So the pattern C D E F G A B fits exactly once around each "two-group then three-group" pair, and then repeats. Every repeat sounds higher (going right) or lower (going left), but a C always sits beside a two-group, wherever it is.

**Middle C.** Your keyboard has several Cs. For a first lesson everyone uses the same one, **middle C**, so that your hand is in the comfortable middle of the keyboard and the tune sounds at a normal singing height. (The tune would still be correct on another C, just higher or lower.)

<!-- la:worked-example -->
### Finding middle C on a 61-key keyboard

*Goal: find middle C reliably, every time, without guessing.*

1. **Find the leftmost key and check whether it is a C.** *Why start at the end?* The ends are the only places on the keyboard you can find without searching. On almost every 61-key keyboard the lowest key is a C. *Check:* look just to the right of that key — the first black keys you meet should be a group of two, which is exactly where a C sits.
2. **Count Cs going right: 1, 2, 3.** The leftmost key is the first C. Move right to the next white key that sits just left of a group of two: that is the second C. The next one is the third C. **The third C from the left is middle C.** *Why count rather than look for "the middle"?* On a 61-key keyboard the key exactly in the centre is a black key, and there is a C six keys to each side of it, so "the C nearest the middle" can fool you. Counting is certain.
3. **Check your answer from the other end.** A 61-key keyboard from C to C has six Cs in total, so middle C should have two Cs to its left and three Cs to its right (the last C being the rightmost key). If those counts come out right, you have it.
4. **Mark it, if you like.** A small piece of masking tape or a removable sticker on middle C saves time for the first few days. Take it off once you can find middle C without it.

*Before reading on: you could have counted white keys instead of Cs. Why are the black-key groups the better landmark?* Because you can see a group of two at a glance from anywhere on the keyboard, whereas counting white keys one by one is slow and easy to get wrong.

If your leftmost key is not a C (some keyboards differ), start from any C you find with the black-key rule and look for markings: many keyboards print "C" names or a middle C symbol above the keys. Makers label middle C differently (some call it C3, some C4), but it is the same key; if unsure, check your keyboard's manual.

## Finger numbers and the C position

You can now find any white key. The tune, though, is not played by one finger hunting from key to key; it is played by a hand that sits still with a finger ready over each note. To describe that, every finger needs a name.

**Finger numbers.** Piano music numbers the fingers of each hand the same way:

| Finger | Number |
|---|---|
| thumb | 1 |
| index (pointing) finger | 2 |
| middle finger | 3 |
| ring finger | 4 |
| little finger | 5 |

The left hand uses the same numbers (its thumb is also 1), but in this lesson you only use your right hand. A quick way to learn them: hold your right hand up and wiggle each finger as you say its number, 1 to 5 and back, three times.

**The C position.** Put your right thumb (1) on middle C. Let the other fingers fall on the next white keys to the right, one finger per key, with no gaps:

| Finger | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Key | C (middle C) | D | E | F | G |

This is called a **five-finger position**, and this one is the **C position** because the thumb is on C. Shift your seat so that you sit roughly opposite middle C. Check the position with the map: your finger 2 should be in the middle of the group of two black keys (D), and your finger 5 should be between the first two black keys of the group of three (G). In this position you can play C, D, E, F and G without moving your hand, and "Mary Had a Little Lamb" uses only C, D, E and G.

**Warm-up: the five-finger walk.** In C position, play C D E F G F E D C with fingers 1 2 3 4 5 4 3 2 1, one note at a time, slowly and evenly — about one note per second. Play only one key at a time: as one finger goes down, the previous one comes up. Do this three times at the start of every session. What to listen for: every note about equally loud and equally long. What to watch for: finger 4 often drags finger 5 or 3 along with it; that is normal, and slow practice is how the fingers learn to move separately.

<!-- la:misconception -->
> **A tempting mistake: "Each letter has its own finger — E is finger 3."** In C position finger 3 does play E, so it is easy to fuse the two in your memory. But the finger is decided by *where your hand sits*, not by the letter. If you moved your thumb to G, finger 3 would land on B, and E would not be under your hand at all. The right model: finger numbers describe your hand's own pattern; the letters depend on where you put that pattern. Keep both in mind: in this lesson's table you get the letter *and* the finger for every note. You will use this idea in the last exercise.

**Two small moves the tune needs.**

- **Playing the same key again (a repeated note).** The tune often plays the same note two, three or four times in a row (E E E). A key makes a new sound only when it has come back up first. So between repeats, let the key rise all the way while your fingertip stays lightly touching its surface, then press again.
- **Moving to a different key.** When going from one note to the next (E to D), aim for the new finger going down at the same moment as the old finger comes up — no silence between them and no overlap. Do not worry if this is uneven at first; the beat matters more right now.

## The beat: how long each note lasts

You have the *where* (the map) and the *which finger* (the C position). What is still missing is the *when*. If you play the right keys at random moments, nobody will recognise the tune; its character comes from when each note starts and how long it lasts. This section gives you the beat, which is what "steadily" really means.

**The beat (or pulse).** Music like this tune rests on a steady, repeating beat, the thing you would tap your foot to. Count it aloud, evenly: "1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4, …". Each group of four beats is called a **bar** (also called a measure). The count starts again at 1 at the start of every bar. "Mary Had a Little Lamb" is 8 bars long, so it lasts 8 × 4 = 32 beats.

**Note lengths.** Each note in the tune starts on a beat and lasts a whole number of beats. Three lengths are used, with their standard names:

| Name | How many beats it lasts | How you count it |
|---|---|---|
| quarter note | 1 beat | press on a count, release as the next count arrives |
| half note | 2 beats | press on a count, keep holding through the next count |
| whole note | 4 beats | press on "1", hold through "2, 3, 4" |

(The names compare lengths: a half note lasts half as long as a whole note, a quarter note a quarter as long. In this tune the beat is one quarter note.)

With a piano sound, a note you hold slowly gets quieter, the way a real piano's does. That is normal: keep holding the key for the full count anyway, and do not press it again.

**Tempo.** The **tempo** is how fast the beat goes, measured in beats per minute. It is often written with a note symbol and a number: **♩ = 60** means 60 quarter-note beats per minute, which is exactly one beat per second. A **metronome** clicks at the tempo you set. Many keyboards have one built in (look for a "Metronome" button and a tempo setting); otherwise, any free metronome app or website will do. At ♩ = 60 the whole tune takes 32 seconds.

**The clock picture, and where it stops working.** A steady beat behaves like the tick of a clock: it keeps going at the same speed whether something interesting is happening or not, and it does not wait for you. That is the part to copy. Where the picture breaks: a clock never changes speed, while real musicians do sometimes slow down on purpose, for example at the very end of a piece. That is a deliberate musical choice, made by someone who could have kept the beat steady. For this lesson, be the clock.

<!-- la:misconception -->
> **A tempting mistake: "Steady means I play each note when I'm ready."** Nearly every beginner does this: the easy repeated notes come out quickly, and then there is a pause before a note that needs a moment's thought (the tune has one such note, a G that you will meet soon). Each pause feels tiny, but the beat stops while you think. The listener hears a hiccup, and the half note before or after the pause gets the wrong length. The fix is not to try harder at the hard spot. **Choose a tempo slow enough for the hardest spot, and play the easy parts at that same tempo.** If the beat has to wait for you anywhere, the tempo is too fast for now.

<!-- la:misconception -->
> **A second tempting mistake: "Once a long note has sounded, it's done."** On a half note, your finger has nothing new to do on the second beat, so it is tempting to let go early and get ready for the next note. That turns the half note into a quarter note and a gap. Keep the key down while you say the second count, and let it rise only as the next note's count arrives.

<!-- la:worked-example -->
### Counting bars 1 and 2 away from the keyboard

*Goal: feel the rhythm of the first two bars before your fingers have to do anything else.* Rhythm first, keys later, because each is easier to learn alone.

Here are bars 1 and 2: **E D C D | E E E**. Their lengths in beats are **1 1 1 1 | 1 1 2** (the last E is a half note).

1. **Set the beat.** Count "1, 2, 3, 4" aloud twice, evenly, at about one count per second, before tapping anything. *Why first?* You need a beat running before you can place notes on it.
2. **Bar 1: four quarter notes.** Tap your right index finger on the table on each count while saying it: tap on "1", tap on "2", tap on "3", tap on "4". *Check:* four taps, one per count, all evenly spaced.
3. **Bar 2: two quarters and a half.** Tap on "1", tap on "2", tap on "3" and keep your finger pressed on the table while you say "4". Lift it as you say the next "1". *Why keep the finger pressed?* That is exactly what you will do on the key, so the half note lasts its full two beats.
4. **Check the arithmetic.** Bar 2 lasts 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 beats, the same as every bar. If your finger came up before you said "4", you lifted early on the half note.
5. **Loop it.** Count and tap bars 1 and 2 three times in a row without a break between repeats.

*Before reading on: in bar 2 nothing new happens on count "4", so why say "4" out loud at all?* Because saying it keeps the beat running. Without it, the half note shrinks and the next bar starts early.

## Learning the melody in short loops

You now have all three ingredients: where each note is, which finger plays it, and when it happens. Here is the complete tune with all three for every note. Then you will learn it in small pieces, because trying to do everything at once, over the whole tune, is how beginners get stuck.

**"Mary Had a Little Lamb", right hand, C position.** The tune is the one commonly sung to the nursery rhyme; it is in the public domain (see the note at the end of the lesson). Each row is one bar of 4 beats.

| Bar | Notes | Fingers | Beats each note lasts | Words (to help you remember) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | E D C D | 3 2 1 2 | 1 1 1 1 | Ma - ry had a |
| 2 | E E E | 3 3 3 | 1 1 2 | lit - tle lamb, |
| 3 | D D D | 2 2 2 | 1 1 2 | lit - tle lamb, |
| 4 | E G G | 3 5 5 | 1 1 2 | lit - tle lamb, |
| 5 | E D C D | 3 2 1 2 | 1 1 1 1 | Ma - ry had a |
| 6 | E E E E | 3 3 3 3 | 1 1 1 1 | lit - tle lamb, its |
| 7 | D D E D | 2 2 3 2 | 1 1 1 1 | fleece was white as |
| 8 | C | 1 | 4 | snow. |

Notice the shape: bars 1 and 5 are identical, and bars 1–4 and 5–8 start the same way but end differently. Bar 4 has the only skip in the tune (E up to G, missing out F), and bar 8 is a single long note.

**The short-loop method.** This is how to learn each piece:

1. **Pick a small chunk**: usually two bars, or just two notes if one move is giving trouble.
2. **Add the next note.** End the chunk on the first note of the following bar, so the join is practised too.
3. **Play it slowly while counting aloud**, at a speed where you never have to pause. Slow enough is slower than you think.
4. **Count only clean repetitions.** A clean repetition has the right notes, the right fingers, and the beat never stops. Aim for **three clean repetitions in a row**. If one goes wrong, the count goes back to zero; slow down slightly and try again.
5. **Then move on** to the next chunk, and afterwards loop the two chunks together.

<!-- la:misconception -->
> **A tempting mistake: "Practising means playing from the beginning every time, and stopping to fix mistakes."** It feels thorough, but count what it actually trains. If bar 4 is your problem and you start from bar 1 every time, you play bars 1–3 again and again (which you can already do) and bar 4 only once per attempt. And every time you stop at the mistake, you rehearse *stopping*, which is the one thing a steady performance cannot have. Short loops do the opposite: the trouble spot gets most of the repetitions, slowly enough to be clean.

<!-- la:worked-example -->
### Learning bars 1–4 at the keyboard

*Goal: play bars 1–4 with the right notes, fingers and counts, three times in a row.* Here is how to apply the loop method, one decision at a time.

1. **Set the C position.** Thumb on middle C, fingers 2, 3, 4, 5 on D, E, F, G. *Check:* finger 2 is between the two black keys; finger 5 is between the first two black keys of the group of three. Wrist level, fingertips on the keys.
2. **Bar 1, notes and fingers only.** Play E D C D with fingers 3 2 1 2, slowly, saying the *finger numbers* aloud: "3, 2, 1, 2". *Why say finger numbers rather than letters?* Your hand stays still, so if the fingers are right the notes are right; saying the numbers catches a wrong finger at once. *Check:* the hand did not move along the keyboard.
3. **Bar 1 with counting, joined to bar 2's first note.** Now count "1, 2, 3, 4" aloud at about one count per second, one note on each count, and add the first E of bar 2 on the next "1". Loop it: E D C D E. Three clean repetitions.
4. **Bar 2: repeated notes and the first half note.** E E E with finger 3, lengths 1 1 2. Let the key rise fully between the Es, as in the "repeated note" move. Hold the last E through "3, 4". Then loop bars 1–2 with the counts until you have three clean repetitions. *Check:* the half note lasts two counts — the same thing you tapped on the table earlier.
5. **Bars 3 and 4, including the skip.** Bar 3 is D D D with finger 2, lengths 1 1 2. Bar 4 is E G G with fingers 3 5 5, lengths 1 1 2.
   *Before reading on: which finger plays the G, and why not finger 4, which is right next to finger 3?* Finger 5, because in C position finger 4 sits over F, and G is under finger 5. Using 4 would mean moving your hand, and then nothing else in the tune would be under the right finger. So finger 4 simply rests above F and does nothing; the skip is a finger choice, not a hand movement.
   Loop bars 3–4 (plus bar 5's first note, E with finger 3) slowly, three clean times. If you hesitate before the G, that is the spot that needs a smaller loop: just E G, E G, counting, until it is easy.
6. **Bars 1–4 together.** Loop the whole first half, counting aloud, until you get three clean repetitions in a row. *Check by ear:* this is the first line of the tune, "Mary had a little lamb, little lamb, little lamb".

## Joining the halves and switching on the metronome

Each half of the tune now works on its own, at your own counting speed. The goal, though, is the whole tune at a steady, external tempo. Two new things can go wrong when you combine: the join between the halves, and keeping up with a metronome that will not wait. Here is how to deal with each.

**The join, bars 4 → 5.** Bar 4 ends with G held for two counts by finger 5. Bar 5 begins with E, finger 3. Finger 3 is already above E, because your hand has not moved, so the join needs no hand movement at all. Release the G exactly as the E goes down on the next "1". Loop bars 4–5 three clean times.

**The whole tune, counting aloud.** Play all 8 bars counting aloud, without a metronome yet, at a speed where nothing needs a pause. Get three clean repetitions in a row before adding the metronome.

**Bringing in the metronome: a tempo ladder.**

1. Set the metronome to **♩ = 50** (a little slower than one click per second). Listen to four clicks while counting along aloud, then start the tune on the next "1".
2. Play the whole tune with the metronome. Each note should start exactly on its click; half notes last two clicks; the final C lasts four clicks.
3. When you get **three clean repetitions in a row** at ♩ = 50, set it to **♩ = 60** and repeat.
4. Speed is not what makes you steady; being steady at a slow tempo is what lets you go faster later. So only move up a step after three clean repetitions, and if things fall apart, go back down a step.

**When combining breaks something.** It usually breaks at one particular place. Find which bar it is, loop that bar with its neighbours (for example bars 3–5) at a slower tempo until it is clean three times, then go back to the whole tune. Do not keep restarting the whole tune and hoping.

**Predict, then record.** Before your first full run at ♩ = 60, write down one prediction: the bar where you think the beat is most likely to slip, and how (a pause, a rush, or a half note cut short). Then record yourself on a phone, with the metronome audible, and listen back. Did each note land on its click? Was your prediction right? Either answer is useful: it tells you which loop to practise next.

If you do not pass, use the recording to find the bar where it went wrong and make a loop plan for it, as in Exercise 5.

**Exercise 7 (same tune, new place).** Put your right thumb on the **G** just above middle C, and let your other fingers fall on the next four white keys. Write down which letter is under each finger. Then work out the letter names of the whole tune in this new position — using the *finger numbers* from the table — and play it, counting aloud at a speed you choose. What does it sound like compared with the C-position version?

## What you can do now

<!-- la:synthesis -->
We started with a silent keyboard and a question: how does someone who has never played get to a whole melody played steadily, without stopping? The answer is that "playing a tune" is really three separate jobs, and each is easy on its own. **Where**: the black-key groups give every white key an address, and middle C gives your hand a home. **Which finger**: in C position each note of the tune already has a finger above it, so your hand never has to move and finger numbers tell you everything. **When**: a steady beat, counted aloud, fixes when each note starts and how long it lasts.

You learnt those jobs one at a time, then combined them in small loops, slowly enough that the beat never had to wait. That last part is the key to "steadily". The beat breaks at the hard spots, so you made the hard spots small, slow and repeated until they were no longer hard, and chose a tempo the whole tune could keep. "Mary Had a Little Lamb" at ♩ = 60, three times without stopping, is what that looks like when it works. If you can now pass Exercise 6, you have done what you set out to do.

You can now find the white-key notes, place your hand in C position, sit and play without strain, count quarter, half and whole notes, play a complete melody steadily, and diagnose and fix your own trouble spots. Exercise 7 showed that the finger pattern is portable: move it, and the same tune comes out from a new starting key. This lesson did not cover reading notes on a staff, the black keys as notes, tunes that need your hand to move, or the left hand.

**A sensible next step:** keep the warm-up and play the tune once a day for a week, then learn a second tune that fits the C position (for example, the traditional "Hot Cross Buns", which uses only E, D and C), or begin a beginner method book that teaches staff reading from middle C.

## Hints and solutions

<!-- la:hint id=E1 -->
**Hint for Exercise 1.** For question 1, keep your eyes on the groups of two black keys and use them to find each C, rather than counting white keys — and check the very last key on the right too. For question 2, do the same with the groups of three.

<!-- la:solution id=E1 -->
**Solution to Exercise 1.**

1. On a standard 61-key keyboard from C to C there are **6 Cs**: one just left of each of the five groups of two black keys, plus the rightmost key, which has no black keys after it but follows the same C D E F G A B pattern (it comes right after a B).
2. There are **5 Fs**, one just left of each group of three. If you got 6, you probably counted the white keys left of the groups of *two*: those are the Cs. Look for three black keys together.
3. From middle C going right: **C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C**. G was the fifth key you named, the white key between the first and second black keys of the group of three.
4. If you found middle C three times by spotting a group of two and checking with the count of Cs, that part is done.

<!-- la:hint id=E2 -->
**Hint for Exercise 2.** Every bar adds up to 4 beats. A note that lasts 2 beats starts on one count and is held through the next.

<!-- la:solution id=E2 -->
**Solution to Exercise 2.**

- **Bar 3 (D D D, 1 1 2):** tap on 1, tap on 2, tap on 3 and hold through 4. Total 1 + 1 + 2 = 4.
- **Bar 4 (E G G, 1 1 2):** the same rhythm as bar 3 (and bar 2): tap on 1, tap on 2, tap on 3 and hold through 4.
- **Bar 8 (C, 4):** tap on 1 and hold through 2, 3 and 4.

The most common mistake is lifting early on the held counts, so bar 8 lasts one or two counts instead of four. You can hear it in one of two ways: if you kept counting, there is a silence before the next "1"; if you stopped counting, the next bar starts too soon. Either way, the fix is to say every count aloud, including the held ones, with your finger still down.

<!-- la:hint id=E3 -->
**Hint for Exercise 3.** Compare each of bars 5–8 with bars 1–4 in the table before you play. Most of it you have already played.

<!-- la:solution id=E3 -->
**Solution to Exercise 3.**

1. Bar 5 is identical to bar 1: E D C D, fingers 3 2 1 2, one beat each.
2. In bar 6 (E E E E, all finger 3, one beat each), each key must come all the way back up before the next press, or the next E will not sound. Keep the fingertip lightly on the key's surface as it rises, so the repeats stay even.
3. Bar 7: D D E D with fingers **2 2 3 2**, one beat each.
4. Bar 8: C with finger **1** (the thumb), held for **4 counts**: press on "1", hold through "2, 3, 4".

A likely error is playing bar 6 as bar 2 (three Es with a half note at the end) because it starts the same. Check the table: bar 6 has four quarter notes, because the words have four syllables, "lit-tle lamb, its".

<!-- la:solution id=E4 -->
**Solution to Exercise 4.** You pass if all the answers are "yes" and nothing moved when you let go (shoulders did not drop, thumb did not spring loose). If your shoulders dropped, they were raised while playing: play the bar again with them deliberately low. If the thumb sprang away, it was being held stiffly: let it rest lightly on its side. A sagging wrist usually means the seat is too low; a high, arched wrist usually means it is too high or you are sitting too close. Adjust the seat first, not the hand. And if anything hurt, stop and rest; see the safety note near the start.

<!-- la:hint id=E5 -->
**Hint for Exercise 5.** For each problem, ask: what is the smallest piece of music that contains the problem? Loop that piece, slowly, then make it bigger.

<!-- la:solution id=E5 -->
**Solution to Exercise 5.** One good plan for each (yours may differ in the details but should have the same parts: a small loop, slow, counted, clean repetitions, then rejoin).

1. **Pause before the G.** The problem is the move from E (finger 3) to G (finger 5). Loop just **E G** counting "1, 2" slowly, until it is three clean in a row; then loop **all of bar 4 plus the first E of bar 5**; then **bars 3–5**; then the whole tune at ♩ = 50 before going back to 60. Remind yourself that the G is under finger 5 already, so there is nothing to find. The common wrong plan is "play the whole tune more times": that keeps the pause, because the pause is being practised every time.
2. **Rushing bar 6.** Repeated notes tempt you to hurry because nothing new has to be found. Loop **bar 6 plus the first D of bar 7** with the metronome at ♩ = 50, *saying the counts aloud* and making each E land on a click, three clean times. Then loop **bars 5–7**, then the whole tune at 50, then 60. Check with a new recording: the four Es should be evenly spaced, each on a click.

<!-- la:hint id=E6 -->
**Hint for Exercise 6.** If one place keeps breaking the run, don't restart from bar 1 each time: loop just that spot with the metronome (as in the tempo ladder), then rejoin the whole tune. If you lose the beat in the rests between repetitions, count the four rest clicks aloud.

<!-- la:solution id=E6 -->
**Solution to Exercise 6.** There is no single "answer" to compare; the recording is your evidence. Listen through once for notes only (any wrong letter?), once for the beat (does every note land on a click, and do the held notes last 2 and 4 clicks?), and once for stops. A run counts only if all three checks pass for all three repetitions. Typical reasons for failing, and the matching fix: a pause before bar 4's G (Exercise 5, plan 1); bar 6 rushing (Exercise 5, plan 2); the final C cut short (count "2, 3, 4" aloud while holding it); a stop between repetitions (practise continuing straight into the four rest clicks). If you pass, you have reached this lesson's goal.

<!-- la:hint id=E7 -->
**Hint for Exercise 7.** The G just above middle C is the key your finger 5 was on in C position. First find the letters under fingers 1 to 5 using the keyboard map. Then, for each note in the table, ignore the letter and use the finger number: which key is that finger on now?

<!-- la:solution id=E7 -->
**Solution to Exercise 7.** In this **G position** the fingers sit over:

| Finger | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Key | G | A | B | C | D |

All five are white keys: G and A sit inside the group of three black keys, B just right of it, C just left of the next group of two, and D in the middle of that group. Translating the table's finger numbers into these letters gives:

| Bar | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fingers | 3 2 1 2 | 3 3 3 | 2 2 2 | 3 5 5 | 3 2 1 2 | 3 3 3 3 | 2 2 3 2 | 1 |
| Notes | B A G A | B B B | A A A | B D D | B A G A | B B B B | A A B A | G |

The lengths in beats are unchanged. It sounds like the **same tune, only higher**, because every note moved up by the same amount. The likely mistake is keeping the C-position letters (playing E, D, C …) with the hand in G position, or keeping finger 3 on E: that is the "each letter has its own finger" idea from earlier. Here finger 3 plays B. What stayed the same is the finger pattern, and that is what your hand had learnt.
